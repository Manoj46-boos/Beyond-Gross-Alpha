### ACT I — THE WRONG SCOREBOARD — 1:35–3:40
*[VISUAL: slow push-in on a real-looking brokerage statement. The "Total Return" line glows. Below it, an empty row where "After-Tax Return" should be.]*

> Here's a number you've never seen: your portfolio's after-tax return. Not because it's secret. Because nobody computes it. Your statement shows performance before tax. Morningstar stars are awarded before tax. Your advisor's pitch deck — before tax. The entire industry keeps score in a currency you can't spend.

> Why? Start with incentives. Cerulli projects that by 2026, eighty-three percent of advisors will charge under one percent on clients with five million or more — average fees heading toward two-thirds of a point. [S2] When the fee compresses, time goes to whatever justifies the fee: planning, estates, insurance, hand-holding. Tax management is expensive to deliver — the software to scan hundreds of accounts daily can cost a small firm five figures a year — and invisible when delivered. Nobody claps for a tax that didn't happen.

*[VISUAL: compliance memo aesthetic — redacted lines, "E&O EXCLUSION" stamp.]*

> Then compliance. Many firms draw a hard legal line between tax planning and tax advice. Cross it without a CPA and the liability is real; errors-and-omissions policies often exclude it. So the rational advisor stays quiet. And then the human part: advisors are people. Loss aversion makes selling a loser feel like failure even when it's a win. Motivated reasoning makes the pre-tax number — the one that sells — feel like the real one.

> To be fair: seventeen percent is not zero. The best firms do this beautifully, usually arm-in-arm with a CPA. And the survey measures what drives decisions, not what gets mentioned at lunch. But the structure is the point. Wealthy families now hold roughly fifty-four percent of US household financial assets [S1] — this is the industry's main event, not a niche. And yet you are scored on gross, your advisor is paid on gross, and the tax line is nobody's job. Unless you make it somebody's.

*[TURN — hold one beat of silence.]*

> So what does that line actually cost? Let's use research, not vibes.

### ACT II — ANATOMY OF THE DRAG — 3:40–5:55
*[G3: Schwab model. Assumptions footnote visible, not hidden: 30 years · 6% capital-gains return · 0% dividends · 2026 top federal rates · excludes state tax.]*

> Schwab's Center for Financial Research modeled a portfolio earning six percent in capital gains, held thirty years, taxed at top federal rates — forty point eight percent on short-term gains, twenty-three point eight on long-term. That's the thirty-seven percent top bracket plus the three-point-eight investment-income surtax. In California, add thirteen point three — long-term gains run past thirty-seven percent there. [S3]

> Now vary one thing: turnover. Sell and replace five percent of the portfolio a year — barely trading — and taxes shave about zero point seven percentage points off your annual return. Turn over the whole portfolio every year — normal for many active funds — and the drag nearly triples, to about one point nine.

*[G3b: two growth curves from $100,000. Labels land: $469,970 vs $330,190.]*

> In dollars: a hundred thousand dollars, same six percent, same thirty years. The low-turnover version ends near four hundred seventy thousand. The high-turnover version: three hundred thirty. A hundred forty thousand dollars — thirty percent of the ending wealth — gone. Not to the market. To the calendar. And that's before state tax.

> Two mechanics drive this. First, every sale realizes a gain — and gains held under a year are taxed like salary. Second, the character of a return matters as much as its size. Bond interest is ordinary income. Most REIT distributions, ordinary. Qualified dividends and long-term gains get the discount. BlackRock's analysis of Morningstar data puts it bluntly: taxable bonds carry a similar five-year tax cost to equities — but on a much smaller pre-tax return. Taxes eat a far bigger share of a bond's return than a stock's. [S4]

*[G4: THE signature graphic. $2M at 8% vs 6%, 25 years. The gap shades gold. This is the thumbnail moment — hold it 4 full seconds.]*

> Now scale it up. Two million, eight percent gross, twenty-five years: thirteen point seven million if the full return compounds. Let two points a year leak — roughly Schwab's high-turnover drag at top rates — and you compound at six: eight point six million. The five point one million from the opening isn't a scare number. It's arithmetic. And the first lever to stop it costs nothing to implement.

### ACT III — LEVER 1: ASSET LOCATION — 5:55–7:45
*[G5: three-column account map builds as spoken: TAXABLE / TAX-DEFERRED / ROTH. Assets slide into columns.]*

> Lever one: asset location. Not allocation — location. Same assets, different accounts. The rule is simple: put the tax-ugly stuff where the IRS can't reach it annually. Taxable bonds, TIPS, REITs, high-turnover active funds — into the IRA, the 401(k), the tax-deferred sleeve. Broad index ETFs and growth stocks — into the taxable account, where low turnover and qualified dividends already tax them gently. And your highest-expected-growth assets belong in the Roth, because that growth is never taxed again.

> Two traps the pros still step in. Municipal bonds in an IRA: you take tax-exempt interest and convert it into fully taxable withdrawals. You've laundered a tax advantage into a tax bill. Munis live in taxable accounts — that's the whole point of them. [S5]

*[VISUAL: the IRA column flips to show "$1,000,000 → $700,000 yours · $300,000 government's."]*

> Trap two: location changes your real risk. A million in a traditional IRA is not a million — at a thirty percent future rate, it's seven hundred thousand of your money and three hundred thousand of the government's. Park all your bonds in the IRA and your taxable account — the one you actually spend from — is now all equity. Your after-tax allocation is riskier than the spreadsheet says.

> How much is location worth? Vanguard's simulations: up to about thirty basis points a year. Goldman Sachs Asset Management models more than forty-five for high brackets when combined with strategic allocation. [S6] Real money — but modest, and capped by how much tax-advantaged space you have. For most of you, most of your wealth is taxable. Which is why the next lever matters more.

### ACT IV — LEVER 2: TAX-LOSS HARVESTING — 7:45–11:05
*[VISUAL: animated mechanic — a red position sells, the loss becomes a gold "tax asset" token, a similar-but-different position replaces it. 61-day window renders as a calendar bracket.]*

> Lever two: tax-loss harvesting. The most studied — and most oversold — tool in the kit. The mechanic: sell a position that's down, bank the loss against gains elsewhere, reinvest immediately in something similar but not substantially identical. You keep the market exposure; the loss becomes a tax asset. Losses offset gains without limit, then up to three thousand dollars of ordinary income a year, and carry forward indefinitely.

> The guardrail is the wash-sale rule: buy the same thing back within thirty days — before or after the sale, a sixty-one-day window — and the loss vanishes. Swapping between two S&P 500 ETFs is common practice, but the IRS has never blessed it; conservative harvesters switch index families entirely. And here's the trap that catches even professionals: trigger a wash sale by repurchasing inside your IRA — or your spouse's — and the loss isn't deferred. It's destroyed. Permanently. The IRS ruled on this in 2008; there's no basis adjustment on the other side. [S7]

*[G6: the FAJ waterfall — 1.08% → 0.82% (wash-sale rule) → 0.95% (after costs, separate adjustment). Then the four era bars: 2.13 / 0.51 / 1.08 / 0.81.]*

> So what is harvesting actually worth? The independent benchmark is a 2020 study in the Financial Analysts Journal — Chaudhuri, Burnham, and Andrew Lo — simulating monthly harvesting on the five hundred largest US stocks from 1926 to 2018. Average alpha: about one point one percent a year. Force it to obey the wash-sale rule: zero point eight. Subtract trading costs: about zero point nine five. [S8]

> And the average hides the story. Two point one percent a year through the Depression's volatility. Zero point five through the calm postwar boom. Harvesting is a volatility strategy. It feeds on dispersion. In a straight-line bull market, it starves.

> The vendors' numbers are bigger — up to two point three percent in one widely cited study. But read the footnote: that study came from Aperio, a firm that sells direct indexing, now owned by BlackRock — and the authors themselves say the result depends on having short-term gains to offset. [S9] Independent research says zero point eight to one. Vendor research says two-plus. Both are real. Only one is disinterested.

*[VISUAL: Wealthfront results page on screen. Then April 2025 tariff-selloff chart, 3-day window highlighted.]*

> What does it look like in production? Wealthfront publishes its results — rare in this industry. In 2025, an estimated one hundred sixty-one million dollars of client tax savings. During the tariff selloff in April, its software harvested a hundred million dollars of losses in three days. [S10] That's the frequency argument in one number: a year-end review would have missed almost all of it.

> But their own data shows the catch. Brand-new accounts harvested losses worth nearly eight percent of portfolio value in 2025. Older accounts: far less. As markets rise, your cost basis rises, and the harvest thins. Tax alpha decays. And the deepest truth: harvesting is deferral, not forgiveness. Every harvested loss lowers your basis — a bigger gain waiting later. It becomes permanent only if those shares are eventually donated, or stepped up at death. More on that in a minute.

*[MUSIC OUT. VISUAL: the actual SEC press release on screen, key lines highlighted. Tell it like a story, slower.]*

> One more warning, from the SEC's own files. In 2023, Betterment paid nine million dollars over its tax-loss harvesting service — coding errors and undisclosed changes that cost more than twenty-five thousand client accounts roughly four million dollars in tax benefits. [S11] The strategy was real. The execution wasn't. Tax alpha you can't verify is just a brochure.

*[Beat. Music returns.]*

### ACT V — LEVER 3: THE WRAPPER — 11:05–13:35
*[VISUAL: side-by-side plumbing diagrams. Mutual fund: redemption → manager sells → gains spray onto all holders. ETF: redemption → in-kind basket swap → no sale.]*

> Lever three: the wrapper. Same index, same stocks — different tax outcome, depending on the legal structure you hold them through. Here's the mechanism most investors never learn. When you redeem a mutual fund, the manager often has to sell holdings to pay you out — and those gains get distributed to everyone who stayed. You can buy a fund in December and owe taxes on gains it realized in March, before you owned a single share.

> An ETF works differently. Redemptions happen in-kind — a large institutional partner swaps ETF shares for a basket of the underlying stocks. No sale. No realized gain. The fund can even hand out its lowest-basis shares on the way out, quietly washing embedded gains out of the structure. The industry calls the bigger versions heartbeat trades — and Bloomberg estimates this machinery now costs the US Treasury around forty-eight billion dollars a year, with the top one percent collecting most of the benefit. [S12] File that away. We'll come back to it.

*[G7: 57% vs 6% bars. Big, clean, unmissable.]*

> The scoreboard: State Street's 2026 analysis found that in 2025, fifty-seven percent of equity mutual funds paid out capital-gains distributions. Among equity ETFs: six percent. [S13] That's not a rounding error. That's a structural moat. And it's about to get wider — Vanguard's patent on pairing an ETF share class with a mutual fund expired in May 2023; dozens of firms have since filed to copy it. [S14]

> What about funds literally labeled tax-managed? Here the evidence is humbler than the marketing. A 2017 White Coat Investor analysis compared Vanguard's tax-managed small-cap fund to its plain small-cap index fund: ten-year after-tax returns of seven point one six versus six point seven six. An edge — but his own verdict was that most of it came from tracking a different index, and the large-cap tax-managed version added essentially nothing over a plain index fund. [S15] The lesson: a low-turnover index fund is already a tax-managed fund. The label matters less than the turnover, the yield, and the wrapper.

*[TURN — the planted payoff, first detonation.]*

> And one honest caveat on the whole ETF story: that forty-eight billion is a policy choice, not a law of physics. Congress has noticed. The wrapper's advantage is real today — but it exists at the pleasure of the tax code. Build on it. Don't worship it.

### ACT VI — LEVER 4: THE ADVANCED TIER — 13:35–16:35
*[VISUAL: private-bank pitch deck aesthetic, slightly over-designed — then a red pen starts annotating it.]*

> Lever four is where the private banks live: direct indexing, exchange funds, and tax-aware long-short. If your net worth is north of five million, someone has pitched you one of these. Let's separate the engineering from the sales deck.

> Direct indexing: instead of one ETF, you own the hundreds of stocks inside it. Why? Because the index can be up while fifty of its members are down — and you can harvest those fifty losses individually. You can also exclude the stock you already work for, and gift your most-appreciated lots to charity. It is genuinely powerful — for the right person. The right person has a high marginal rate, ongoing gains to offset — a business sale, a concentrated position unwinding — and fresh cash coming in.

> The costs, from Schwab's own disclosure: thirty to forty basis points a year, versus a few basis points for a broad index ETF. Hundreds of cost-basis lots to administer. And — their words — diminishing tax benefits over time as the portfolio appreciates. [S16] How fast does it decay? In one study's simulations, the tax benefit averaged sixty-nine basis points a year in years one through five — and nine basis points in years six through ten. [S21] Pay forty basis points forever to harvest a benefit that decays: that's the trade. Run the math on your situation, not the brochure's.

*[VISUAL: one giant stock block shatters into a diversified mosaic — then a 7-year padlock closes over it.]*

> Exchange funds solve a different problem. BlackRock estimates over a trillion dollars of US household wealth sits in low-basis single stock — founders and early employees who can't diversify without a tax event. [S17] You contribute your concentrated shares into a private pool alongside other people's concentrated shares, and get back a diversified basket carrying your old basis. No tax today. The price: typically a seven-year lockup, a qualified-purchaser gate — five million in investments — a slice of the fund parked in illiquid assets like real estate to satisfy the tax rules, and fees on top. [S18] It's deferral with handcuffs. Sometimes exactly right.

> And the exotic one: tax-aware long-short. Long a basket, short another, harvesting losses from both sides in any market. The engineering is real: a peer-reviewed 2020 study in the Financial Analysts Journal found the structure more tax-efficient than comparable long-only active strategies — and later simulations found the tax benefit can be worth more than double the pre-tax return it sacrifices. [S21]

*[TURN — the strongest counterpoint in the video. Slow down.]*

> But in April 2026, Elm Wealth — the quant firm founded by Victor Haghani of Long-Term Capital Management fame — ran the numbers on the typical pitch. Base case: fees consumed more than half the tax benefit, and the investor ended with a lower after-tax expected return than if she had just sold the stock, paid the tax, and bought an index fund. Even granting the manager half a point of stock-picking alpha, it was roughly a coin flip. [S19]

> Their exceptions are telling. It can make sense if a step-up at death is near — or you're about to move from a high-tax state to a low-tax one. Notice what those exceptions have in common: the strategy isn't creating returns. It's moving gains across time and tax rates. Which brings us to the quietest, most powerful fact in this entire video.

### ACT VII — THE SYNTHESIS — 16:35–19:20
*[MUSIC softens. VISUAL: clean, slow, almost still. This act is the payoff for every loop.]*

> Here it is: the best tax strategy in America is often patience. Under current law, assets in your taxable account held until death get a stepped-up basis — every embedded gain, wiped. The 2025 tax law set the estate exemption at fifteen million per person, thirty per couple, starting 2026 — so for most of you watching, there's no estate tax either. [S20] Donate appreciated shares instead of cash, and the gain disappears too, with a deduction on top.

> Which means a plain, low-turnover index ETF — held for decades, donated or inherited — is already one of the most tax-efficient structures ever designed. Its drag is roughly the tax on its dividends: at today's yields, about a third of a point a year. Before you buy complexity, beat that benchmark. Most pitches can't.

*[G8: THE HIERARCHY — full frame, designed for screenshots. Hold 5 seconds. No VO over the first 2 seconds of it.]*

> So here's the hierarchy. Screenshot this. Tier one, do first: asset location, and systematic, wash-sale-aware loss harvesting across every account you own. Combined, the independent evidence supports something like a quarter to a full point a year — more in volatile markets, less in calm ones, decaying as portfolios age. Tier two: the wrapper. ETFs or low-turnover index funds in taxable; ordinary-income assets sheltered; tax-managed labels optional. Tier three, situational: direct indexing, exchange funds, long-short — only with a specific problem to solve. A concentration. A liquidity event. Gains that need offsetting. Complexity is a cost you pay every year for a benefit that may not show up every year.

*[G9: THE FIVE QUESTIONS — full frame, numbered, screenshot-designed. Each question highlights as spoken.]*

> And the five questions. Send these to whoever manages your money. One: show me my after-tax return for the last three years. Two: what did distributions cost me last year, in dollars? Three: where is my asset location written down — and why? Four: how often do you scan for losses, across which accounts — including my spouse's and my IRAs — and who checks the wash sales? Five: if I liquidated today, what's the embedded tax bill, and what's the plan to shrink it?

> A good advisor answers all five without flinching. A great one already sent you the answers. If yours stalls on question one, you've learned something worth more than this video.

*[VISUAL: return to G1 — the split statement, $13.7M vs $8.6M. Full circle.]*

> Tell me in the comments what happens when you ask — I read them all. And if you know someone who brags about their returns every December, send them this.

*[Beat. Music out for the final two lines.]*

> Gross returns are what you brag about. After-tax returns are what you spend. The market doesn't keep score of what you earned. Only what you kept.
